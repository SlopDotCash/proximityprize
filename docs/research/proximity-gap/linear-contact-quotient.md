# List bounds from intersections of the coefficients of a linear differential equation

At length $n=2^{30}$, every ball on the **first named prime at rate $1/16$** contains at most $n$ words through **806,056,371 errors**. At **rate $1/8$**, every ball on either named prime contains at most $n$ words through **695,116,763 errors**. This gives a first-failure floor for both primes, whose respective budgets are $n$ and $2n$. These bounds hold at every positive interleaving width on any injective grid over the respective prime. The proof gives a local intersection inequality for a differential equation of derivative degree one. [Exact integer and rational certificate](../../../scripts/probes/probe_linear_contact_quotient.py)

## A contact order forces a plane intersection

Work over the algebraic closure of the scalarized alphabet. Write $d=k-1$ and give $X,Y,V$ weights $1,d,d-1$. Consider an irreducible equation $g=A(X,Y)+B(X,Y)V$ of weight $D$, with $B\ne0$. Its coefficients have no common nonconstant factor. At a received point $(x,y)$ put $U=X-x$, $L=Y-y$ and let $o$ be the order in $u$ of $g(x+u,y+uz,z+uw)$. Let $I$ be the local intersection multiplicity of $A,B$ there, taking zero if either is a unit. The case $A=0$ gives $g$ proportional to $V$ and can be included among the degree-one factors directly.

For $o\ge2$, put $m=o-1$. The coefficient of $w$ shows that $B$ vanishes to order at least $m$ in $(U,L)$. The constant coefficient then gives the same assertion for $A$. Their homogeneous degree-$m$ terms satisfy
$$
                   U A_m+L B_m=0.
$$
Indeed $A_m(1,z)+zB_m(1,z)=0$ by the order-$m$ condition.

Compute the dimension of the quotient by $A,B$ and $(U,L)^{2m}$. Before imposing $A,B$ its dimension is $m(2m+1)$. All their multiples are generated by two spaces of multipliers of degree less than $m$, together of dimension $m(m+1)$. When $m\ge2$ their map to this quotient by $(U,L)^{2m}$ already has $m-1$ independent kernel vectors. They are $(UH,LH)$ as $H$ ranges over homogeneous polynomials of degree $m-2$. Thus
$$
                    I\ge m^2+m-1=o^2-o-1 .
$$
For $m=1$ both functions vanish and $I\ge1$. For every integer $j\ge2$ and every $o\ge0$ it follows that $I\ge 2jo-j(j+1)-1$. At $o\ge2$ subtract the two bounds and use $(o-j)(o-j-1)\ge0$.

Put $a=\lfloor(D-d+1)/d\rfloor$ and $b=\lfloor D/d\rfloor$. Summing over the grid gives
$$
  2j\sum_x o_x \le [j(j+1)+1]n+aD+b(D-d+1)-dab.                 \tag{1}
$$
To see the degree bound, first use the actual $Y$-degrees $a_0=\deg_Y B$ and $b_0=\deg_Y A$. The Sylvester determinant in $Y$ is nonzero. Its terms contain $a_0$ coefficients of $A$, $b_0$ of $B$, and their total index weight is $a_0b_0$. Its $X$-degree is at most $a_0D+b_0(D-d+1)-da_0b_0$. This expression is nondecreasing up to the two stated integer caps.

At each chosen abscissa, the order of the determinant is at least $I$. Over the discrete valuation ring at $x$, its order is the length of the cokernel of the Sylvester matrix. This cokernel maps onto the local intersection algebra. Indeed the output polynomials of degree below $a_0+b_0$ span the latter modulo $X-x$, since at least one specialized coefficient polynomial is nonzero and has degree at most $\max(a_0,b_0)$. Nakayama's lemma gives surjectivity, and all rows representing multiples of $A,B$ map to zero. If both $Y$-degrees vanish, coprimality rules out a common zero. This proves (1), including degree drops on specialization.

## Excluding large groups on high-degree factors

Use the local rank $C(r,h)$, source size $N(w,h)$ and group variance bound from the [uniform quotient proof](sixteenth-list-quotient-jets.md). The source uses strict weight cutoff $w$ and allows $V$-degree at most $h$. Scalarization makes the argument apply to every interleaving width. Take $t=n-e$, with $e$ the safe error count above, and use primary order $r_0=5$ at rate $1/16$ or $6$ at rate $1/8$, both with derivative cap two. Write $P=r_0t$, $b_0=\lfloor(P-1)/(d-1)\rfloor$. Positive primary surplus gives a nonzero polynomial vanishing on all candidate jets. There are at most two distinct factors of positive derivative degree. Factors independent of $V$ account for at most $b_0$ words.

Use a large-group threshold $L=n/4$ at sixteenth rate and $L=n/8$ at eighth rate. On a factor of derivative degree two, removing the intersections with its $V$-derivative retains at least $M_0=L-b_0^2$ regular words. With $s=t/n$ and $R=d/n$, the uniform quotient proof bounds the mean contact order by
$$
       1+(D-d+1)/t+(D/d+2)\sqrt{R-s^2+(s-R)/M_0}/(2s).
$$
For a linear factor use (1) instead. For either derivative degree $v$ the strict inequality
$$
 N(rt,h)-N(rt-D,h-v)>n\{C(r,h)-C(r,h-v)
         +[C(r,h-v)-C(r-1,h-v)]\overline o\}                      \tag{2}
$$
produces a secondary polynomial coprime to the factor. It vanishes at the generic jets of the group. Their number exceeds the product of the two total degrees, contrary to Bézout. The successive increments of the local rank are nondecreasing because multiplication by $X-x$ injects one order-graded piece into the next.

Take $r=6000$. For $v=2$, verify (2) at $D=2(d-1)$ and $D=P-1$ with $h=1800$. For $v=1$ the lower endpoints are $6d-1$ at sixteenth rate and $5d-1$ at eighth rate. Verify every weight from this lower endpoint to $P-1$. Split it wherever $a$ or $b$ in (1) changes. On each interval the verifier finds one $j$ and one $h$ for which both endpoint surpluses are positive. Each surplus is concave there, since the subtracted source size is a sum of positive parts of affine functions of $D$ and the mean bound is affine. The least certified linear endpoint surplus is 44,891,745,487,161 at rate $1/16$ and 86,101,422,068,475 at rate $1/8$. Thus a large group lies on a linear factor of total $(Y,V)$-degree at most five or four, respectively. A monomial of total degree $b$ on such a factor has weight at least $bd-1$.

## Counting the remaining words

For a linear differential factor of total degree $b$, the [normalized curve bound](first-order-normalized-curve.md#the-derivative-on-the-smooth-curve) gives at most $b+(2b-2)d$ distinct polynomial solutions if the $(d+1)$-st derivative of $Y$ on the curve is nonzero. If it vanishes, the family incidence bound is $(n-d)/(t-d)$. There are no singular solution jets over the generic field because $A,B$ are coprime there.

At eighth rate there is at most one large linear factor. Indeed, if two exist, take their squarefree product $g$. It divides the primary interpolant, has derivative degree two, and $g_V$ shares neither factor. On the union of the two groups discard their intersections with $g_V$, at most $b_0^2$ words on each. The quadratic variance bound therefore applies to the product with the stated $M_0$. A secondary interpolant satisfying (2) vanishes on both groups. Each group has more words than the Bézout bound, so each factor divides the secondary interpolant. This contradicts its construction outside the global multiples of their product. Consequently all words together number at most $4+6d+L+b_0<n$ at eighth rate. This bound uses one other group of size below $L$ and the primary factors independent of $V$.

At sixteenth rate let $G=5+8d$. If at most one factor carries a large group of total degree five, the whole list has at most $G+\max(n/4,4+6d)+b_0$ words, within the sixteenth-rate budget. Otherwise both distinct linear factors have degree five and at least $n/4$ solutions each. Each divides **every** primary interpolant by Bézout since $n/4>b_0^2$. Their product exhausts the derivative degree. If seven distinct words occur off both factors, then for every primary polynomial its remaining $V$-independent quotient is divisible by the seven corresponding factors $Y-f(X)$. All these primary polynomials therefore share a divisor of weight at least $2(5d-1)+7d=17d-2$. Primitive factorization over the polynomial ring gives this divisibility before inverting $X$-polynomials. The primary kernel would have dimension at most $N(P-17d+2,0)$. Its lower bound $N(P,2)-nC(r_0,2)$ strictly exceeds this, on this input. There are at most six exterior words, so the entire list has at most $2G+6=n$. The geometric proof is written, and its finite comparisons are reproducible with the linked certificate.
